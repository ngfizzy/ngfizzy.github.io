Monday Check-in: If You Cannot Explain the Work, You Do Not Own It

AI-assisted output remains the responsibility of the person who presents and ships it.

Over the weekend, a video circulated of a Canadian politician reading AI-generated text verbatim, including the line: “Here is a tighter version…”

It is easy to mock the individual, but almost everybody uses AI now. Some are simply more careless with it than others.

Leaving politics aside, I think the incident points to two bigger problems.

  1. Lack of ownership

When I watched the video, one thought kept returning: How can someone be so absent-minded while reading a piece of work that is supposedly theirs?

The problem was not simply that AI wrote it. The speaker seemed detached from the material. There was no pause. No recognition that the sentence did not belong. No sense of ownership over what was being presented.

  1. Lack of comprehension

Back in elementary school, we were tested on reading comprehension. We would read a passage, answer questions, return to what we had not understood, and summarise it in our own words.

The point was not merely to prove that we could read. It was to prove that we understood.

Today, I see more presentations where people read verbatim and appear to have little understanding of their own work.

The problem is not limited to speeches and essays. It also appears in code.

AI often produces code that works but is verbose, overly abstract, and exhausting to review. That is one reason people are tempted to stop reviewing AI-generated code and simply merge it.

I recently had Codex build a cyclomatic-complexity tool that forces it to identify complex code, simplify it, and fix its own output. It is one of a growing library of custom tools in my coding harness that I may make public soon.

Initially, I thought the behaviour in that video was simply poor work ethic. But the comprehension problem may also be a symptom of something else: The cognitive overload created by AI verbosity.

The verbosity problem of working with agents is not discussed enough. As a heavy user, I often have to slow down and parse what an agent is saying.

After several paragraphs, I sometimes ask: “So you mean we should do XYZ? Why did that require so many words?”

When every response is long and polished, it becomes easy to skim instead of understand. Once you start skimming, it becomes easier to approve work you do not truly comprehend.

The answer is not to ban AI. Affluent people have always had secretaries and personal assistants. AI has extended that privilege to almost everyone.

But whatever your assistant produces remains your responsibility.

If a CEO signs a bad deal because they trusted their assistant too much, the consequences still belong to the CEO. The same applies to AI.
