Friday Checkout: Thoughts

Racing thoughts on models, work, and what comes next.

As I got ready to draft today's checkout, I had too many thoughts competing for the same post.

So instead of pretending they form one neat argument, I’m calling this one Thoughts.

Maybe I’ll deep dive on each of them over the next few weeks, we'll see.

1. I was wrong

After Opus 5.5, my perspective changed.

Yes, these models still hallucinate confidently. I spent last Friday writing about exactly that.

But holy shit, the things I’ve been able to build lately to make my life easier at work.

And it is fast AF.

At some point you have to update your beliefs when the thing in front of you changes.

Mine have changed.

2. Agents simulate personalities—and those personalities shape their output.

The GPT-6.1 class of models writes some of the ugliest code I have read in my career.

But will it do the job correctly?

Almost every time.

It tends to be literal. It optimises for correctness. It rarely tries to infer the thing I probably wanted but forgot to ask for.

Ask it to be proactive and it sometimes swings too far in the other direction.

Claude 5.5 feels almost opposite.

You can give it half a thought and it will often return something much more complete than what you gave it.

I think this is partly why Claude tends to be better at UI work by default. There is more willingness to infer intent and fill in the gaps.

The downside is that every now and then it will do something beautifully and, in the process, quietly ruin something else.

Perhaps that is part of the cost of creativity. Some amount of exploration means some amount of unpredictability.

For a non-engineer trying to make software, I would probably still recommend Claude.

GPT will frustrate you if you don’t know how to specify what you want.

For me, the answer has increasingly been: use both.

3. Let them prompt each other

Codex is still my daily driver.

In my "AGENTS.md", I explicitly tell Codex to use Claude for things like design exploration and code review, while Codex remains responsible for correctness and implementation.

You do not necessarily need some elaborate multi-agent framework for this.

The agents already know how to talk to other tools.

Sometimes the simplest orchestration is just:

“Ask Claude.”

4. If you are paying for these tools and only using them to code, you are leaving a lot on the table

Get a cheap laptop.

Leave it running at home.

Install the desktop agent.

Install the companion mobile app.

Start thinking about all the routine digital work in your life that requires a computer but not necessarily you sitting in front of that computer.

I won’t say much more.

Let your imagination run.

5. “My job is safe, yours is not” is probably the wrong argument

If we are still debating which specific office job AI will touch, I think we are asking the wrong question.

Most of them will be touched.

And that probably means the days of very narrow responsibilities start becoming harder to justify.

You cannot only know how to code.

You may need to understand customers.

If you work with customers, you may increasingly need to understand what an API is, interrogate data, or run a SQL query even if an agent writes the SQL for you.

The boundaries between roles are already becoming easier to cross.

That means the useful question becomes:

What else can you learn to own?

6. It is okay to mourn

I can imagine what it feels like to watch a machine independently perform something you spent twenty years learning to do.

Recently, I turned on computer use in Codex and watched it operate the AWS console for a personal project, configuring IAM roles accurately.

That was strange to watch....

But this kind of disruption is not new.

My mum used to be a secretary and was very good with a typewriter.

I am old enough to remember computers arriving in government ministries and people like her having to learn an entirely different way of working.

The old skill did not suddenly become worthless, but the environment around it changed.

You could still love horses after the internal combustion engine arrived. You just could not assume the economy would continue organising itself around horses and bulls.

I think we are somewhere inside another transition like that.

7. This is an expensive revolution

Tokens are not free; and I’m already starting to see the inequality hidden inside this new way of working.

Someone who can afford several hundred euros a month in models, compute and tools simply has access to more capability than someone who cannot.

Unless inference becomes dramatically cheaper, that advantage compounds.

Maybe there is a hardware breakthrough, maybe competition drives the price down, maybe this eventually becomes infrastructure that governments treat more like electricity or internet access? I don’t know.

But if access to intelligence becomes an economic input, then who can afford how much of it starts to matter a lot.

8. What worries me is not actually job loss

Maybe this is because of my background.

I would not describe myself as someone who came from privilege, so most of my life has involved repeatedly asking:

What can I do next?

That instinct makes the job-loss question less frightening to me than something else.

What I’m increasingly worried about is preserving my ability to generate independent thought rather than becoming very good at auditing thoughts generated for me.

I had a conversation about this with Igbanam Ogbuluijah months ago and, at the time, I don’t think I fully understood his point.

I do now.

In the corporate world, everybody’s Claude is already starting to talk to everybody else’s Claude by proxy.

Someone generates a proposal, someone else asks their AI to summarise it, a third person asks their AI to review that summary.

Eventually humans are mostly selecting, approving and rejecting options produced somewhere inside this loop.

What happens to the ability to originate an idea?

Skills decay when we stop exercising them.

We already have small examples of this with navigation. Give someone turn-by-turn directions for long enough and they can become surprisingly bad at navigating places they have been many times before.

What happens when we do that to reasoning?

What happens when the default way of encountering a difficult problem becomes asking a model to generate the possible interpretations before we have formed one ourselves?

That worries me far more than whether AI can write code.

Because in a world where people mostly audit options produced by their AI, whoever shapes those options has enormous influence over what people subsequently think is possible.

Maybe I’m overthinking it.

Maybe this is my own AI-psychosis era.

Time will tell.

But these are the questions bouncing around my head right now.

And that’s Friday Checkout.
