---
title: "Monday Check-In: We Use AI at Work but Don’t Allow It in Our Hiring Process."
date: "Late July 2026"
description: "Hiring in the AI era should test systems thinking, production reasoning, and genuine understanding."
---

An ex-colleague recently told me about his experience in the current job market.

He has been searching for a while, and from the interviews he has attended, it seems companies are still confused about how to hire in the age of AI.

Some allow candidates to use AI. Some ban it completely.

What frustrates him most are companies that use AI heavily in their day-to-day work but refuse to allow it during interviews.

Here’s what I told him:

Everyone is trying to figure this out.

Just as individuals are questioning which of their skills remain valuable, organizations are also trying to determine what they should still be hiring for.

What looks like hypocrisy or stupidity from the outside may simply be a reflection of a company’s internal struggle to understand who it is in this new era.

My team recently went through a hiring round, and what we assessed was very different from what I was tested on when I joined about a year ago.

A year ago, my interview included:

* A mental system-design problem that increased progressively in complexity
* A coding exercise
* Questions about infrastructure

This time, when interviewing senior engineers, we focused less on writing code from scratch. Instead, we had a one-hour conversation about challenges candidates had faced in previous roles and how they solved them.

We also used mental system-design exercises to test how they reason about real production environments. Some of the scenarios were based on problems our team had encountered and solved in the past.

Optionally, we included a small debugging session: usually fewer than 50 lines of code, examined on the spot, to see whether the candidate had actually debugged software before.

Interestingly, it was often easy to detect when someone was relying too heavily on AI.

There is a particular cadence to AI-generated answers. You can hear when someone is reading a polished response they do not fully understand. The moment you ask a follow-up question that requires them to go one level deeper, the answer begins to fall apart.

In this new era, I think we should be screening more for systems thinking.

Not the old-school, tool-shopping version of system design, where candidates memorize architectures and reach for familiar technologies on cue.

We should be testing whether someone can understand constraints, reason through trade-offs, investigate ambiguity, and make good decisions in messy production environments. These are the abilities that remain valuable whether a candidate uses AI or not.
