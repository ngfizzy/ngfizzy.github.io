Monday Check-in: The Cold Start

Turning off AI for a while can reveal whether you still understand the craft beneath the tools.

I ran out of my Codex five-hour quota, thanks to Sol, and then did something I had not tried in a while:

I continued coding by hand.

For the first five minutes, I froze.

I stared at the IDE, scrolled up and down, and felt like I had forgotten how to begin. Then I told myself:

I know how to do this.

So instead of prompting Codex, I prompted myself.

“What is the entry point of this feature?”

I went to the controller.

Then I asked myself what I needed to understand to see what was missing.

I started reading the code one line at a time. Command-click to jump to a definition. Command-click to go back. Read. Edit. Search for something. Land in the documentation. Read the documentation. Return to the IDE.

Within ten minutes, I was deep in the code.

About an hour later, my limit reset, and I handed the work back to Codex.

I took three lessons from the experience.

1. I would have called the entire exercise a waste of time if it had not felt so good.

I could feel the code flowing from my mind to my hands.

For that hour, the code actually belonged to me.

2. As long as you properly review the output of your AI, you are unlikely to forget how to code.

I mean an actual code review, not a quick scan before clicking approve.

There may be a cold start. A slight drag when you try to move an idea from your head to your fingertips.

I think that resistance comes partly from your brain knowing there is a faster alternative available.

But once you convince yourself that there is no alternative and sit with the discomfort for a few minutes, the flow begins to return.

A programmatic mind does not suddenly lose its way of thinking.

3. I could continue from where Codex stopped because I had already been programming for nine years before leaning heavily into agentic coding.

What happens to the generation that begins with these tools?

Perhaps it would matter less if they remained cheap and widely accessible. But they are becoming increasingly expensive.

GPT-5.6 Sol, on high reasoning, burns through my limits like a powerful vacuum cleaner. I quickly learned to switch to Terra or reduce the reasoning level to medium, which often brings the experience closer to GPT-5.5 anyway.

All in all, I am not excited about a future where creating software increasingly means enriching the pockets of a few billionaires.

But there is still a choice.

You can turn off the AI sometimes and learn the craft.

Or you can allow your ability to build to remain permanently dependent on someone else’s compute.
