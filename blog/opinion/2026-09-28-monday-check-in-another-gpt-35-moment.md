I had another GPT-3.5 moment last week.

Opus 5.5 is good. Saying it is good almost feels like an understatement.

But this time, the feeling wasn’t dread.

It was expectation about what comes next.

I think the safety of saying “I’ll let AI write the code, but I’ll still decide the architecture” is going to become increasingly uncomfortable.

Any decision that can be represented as text can also become training data. And if it can become training data, there is at least a path towards models getting better at making that decision too.

So I don’t think capability is the most interesting question anymore.

The economic and ethical viability is.

These systems are getting incredibly capable, but the infrastructure behind them is enormous. Training and serving them requires increasingly large amounts of compute, power and cooling.

There are also physical constraints.

We can keep improving chips, architectures and data centres, but computation still eventually runs into physics: heat, energy, communication latency and limits on how far transistor scaling can continue.

Maybe recursive self-improvement changes the economics significantly.

Maybe better models help us design better chips, better training methods and much cheaper inference.

I don’t know.

And separating what is technically plausible from what is marketing, fundraising or genuine research progress is becoming harder.

But there is another limit I’ve run into personally: oversight.

Over the past few weeks, I experimented more with vibe coding because I was sick and busy with other things.

I let agents run for longer stretches with very little supervision.

The results were underwhelming.

I came back to over-engineered implementations and research reports that confidently led to dead ends. Some of those problems only became obvious when I sat down this weekend and manually verified what had actually been done.

So despite how capable these models are becoming, I’m not yet convinced we are at the point where you can remove experienced professionals from the loop and simply keep the agents running.

At least not for work where the result actually matters.

For now, knowing what to ask, what to inspect, what looks suspicious and when to stop the agent still matters a lot.

But boy, these new models are capable.

I’m excited.

And increasingly curious about what exactly my job looks like when the next few generations arrive.

And that’s Monday Check-in.
